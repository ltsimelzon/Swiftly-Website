01 — Who we are

This is Swiftly

Six of us, one workshop, and a car that does not exist yet. That is the interesting part.

Swiftly is a STEM Racing team. We design, simulate, machine and race a miniature CO2-powered Formula 1 car — and we are setting out to do it properly rather than quickly.

Most of what decides this competition happens long before race day. The car is judged against a technical regulation measured in millimetres and grams. The design is interrogated by engineers who want to see the CAD, the analysis and the toolpaths. The project is judged on how it was run. We would rather be strong across all of that than fast and thin.

So we are building the team around evidence: simulate before we cut, measure every change, write down what we learn, and never carry a decision we cannot defend. Six people is small enough that everyone owns something real and large enough that nothing gets dropped.

02 — How we work

Six rules we hold ourselves to

Anyone can call themselves innovative. These are the working habits we are actually committing to — and the ones we expect to be held to.

  1. 01

    Simulate before we cut

    Material is slow and finite; compute is fast and cheap. Every shape earns its place in analysis before it earns time on the machine.

  2. 02

    Change one thing at a time

    A car that got faster for six reasons at once has taught us nothing. Small, isolated changes are the only ones we can actually learn from.

  3. 03

    Design for the machine

    A render is not a car. If a surface cannot be reached by a cutter or held without flexing, it is not a design — it is a wish.

  4. 04

    Chase the whole scoresheet

    Speed is one category among several. The portfolio, the presentation and the pit display are engineering problems too, and we treat them that way.

  5. 05

    Write it down as it happens

    Reconstructing a decision three months later loses the reasoning that made it good. The portfolio is a by-product of working properly, not a task at the end.

  6. 06

    Defend every decision

    If we cannot explain why a part is that shape, it does not go on the car. Judges ask exactly this question, and so should we.

04 — The challenge

What we have entered

STEM Racing — known as F1 in Schools until 2024 — is a global STEM competition founded in 1999 and now running in more than 60 countries, supported by Formula 1. Teams of three to six students design, build, brand and race a miniature car.

Where the points actually are

01

Specifications & engineering

The car is measured against the regulations in parc fermé with purpose-made gauges, and the design is interrogated in an interview covering CAD, CAM, analysis and machining.

02

Portfolios & pit display

An engineering portfolio and an enterprise portfolio, judged alongside a pit display the team designs and builds itself.

03

Presentation & racing

A timed verbal presentation to a panel of judges — and the racing itself, run as knockout rounds where reaction time counts.

05 — Where this goes

The road ahead

We know exactly what the route looks like. We are at the start of it.

  1. Stage one

    Regional Final

    Teams register for their local Regional Final and compete against other teams from their area. This is where we start.

  2. Stage two

    National Final

    Regional winners progress to the UK National Final.

  3. Stage three

    Aramco STEM Racing World Finals

    The overall National Finals winners represent their country at the World Finals, held in a different location each year and usually staged alongside a Formula 1 Grand Prix. That is the target.
